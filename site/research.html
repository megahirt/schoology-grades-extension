Research & sources

The studies behind the design

Every claim on this site points here. Each source was checked, and each has a DOI link to the original. The one-line notes are plain-language summaries. Please read the originals for the details and limits.

The extension itself hasn't been studied. These sources are why it's designed the way it is.

  1. Bergman, P., & Chan, E. W. (2021). Leveraging parents through low-cost technology: The impact of high-frequency information on student achievement. Journal of Human Resources, 56(1), 125–158. doi:10.3368/jhr.56.1.1118-9837R1Weekly alerts to parents about missed assignments, grades and absences cut course failures by 27% and raised attendance by 12%, most for high schoolers and lower-GPA students. J-PAL summary.
  2. Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. (2008). Parent involvement in homework: A research synthesis. Review of Educational Research, 78(4), 1039–1101. doi:10.3102/0034654308325185Setting rules about homework had the most positive link of the strategies studied. Results for direct help were mixed.
  3. Grolnick, W. S., & Ryan, R. M. (1989). Parent styles associated with children's self-regulation and competence in school. Journal of Educational Psychology, 81(2), 143–154. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.81.2.143Support for children's independence, plus parental involvement and structure, went with better self-regulation and competence.
  4. Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin, 125(6), 627–668. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627Rewards tied to doing or finishing tasks lowered intrinsic motivation, more so for children. Positive, informative feedback raised it.
  5. Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. Review of Educational Research, 77(1), 81–112. doi:10.3102/003465430298487Feedback about the task and how to do it better works best. Feedback about the self works least.
  6. Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. Psychological Bulletin, 119(2), 254–284. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254Feedback helped on average, but more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, especially when they drew attention to the self.
  7. Harkin, B., Webb, T. L., Chang, B. P. I., Prestwich, A., Conner, M., Kellar, I., Benn, Y., & Sheeran, P. (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence. Psychological Bulletin, 142(2), 198–229. doi:10.1037/bul0000025Across 138 experiments, monitoring progress helped people reach goals. The effect was larger when progress was recorded or reported.
  8. Bandura, A., & Schunk, D. H. (1981). Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 41(3), 586–598. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586Children working toward near, concrete goals progressed faster and gained more confidence and interest.
  9. Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1Planning when and where to act strongly improved follow-through.
  10. Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. American Psychologist, 55(1), 68–78. doi:10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68Motivation thrives when people feel competent, autonomous and connected. Control and some rewards undermine it.
  11. Silverman, J., & Barasch, A. (2023). On or off track: How (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions. Journal of Consumer Research, 49(6), 1095–1117. doi:10.1093/jcr/ucac029Highlighting a broken streak lowered people's later engagement, more so when they blamed themselves for the break.
  12. Martin, A. J., & Liem, G. A. D. (2010). Academic personal bests (PBs), engagement, and achievement: A cross-lagged panel analysis. Learning and Individual Differences, 20(3), 265–270. doi:10.1016/j.lindif.2010.01.001Personal-best goals, measured against your own past performance, predicted later engagement.
  13. Mueller, C. M., & Dweck, C. S. (1998). Praise for intelligence can undermine children's motivation and performance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 75(1), 33–52. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33Praising effort helped children persist after setbacks. Praising intelligence did not.
  14. Hanus, M. D., & Fox, J. (2015). Assessing the effects of gamification in the classroom: A longitudinal study on intrinsic motivation, social comparison, satisfaction, effort, and academic performance. Computers & Education, 80, 152–161. doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019A course with badges, a leaderboard and coins ended the semester with lower motivation and lower final exam scores.
  15. Jivet, I., Scheffel, M., Drachsler, H., & Specht, M. (2017). Awareness is not enough: Pitfalls of learning analytics dashboards in the educational practice. In Data Driven Approaches in Digital Education (EC-TEL 2017), LNCS 10474, 82–96. doi:10.1007/978-3-319-66610-5_7Dashboards that compare learners with each other tend to push competition over learning. Comparing with goals and your own past works better.
